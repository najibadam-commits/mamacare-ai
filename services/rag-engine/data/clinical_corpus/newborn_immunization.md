# Newborn and Infant Immunization Schedule (NPHCDA)

## Why vaccinate
Vaccines protect babies from serious diseases such as tuberculosis, polio, measles, pneumonia, meningitis and diarrhoea caused by rotavirus.
Routine childhood vaccines are free at government health facilities in Nigeria.

## Routine schedule
- At birth: BCG, oral polio vaccine OPV0 and hepatitis B birth dose.
- At 6 weeks: Pentavalent 1, OPV1, pneumococcal vaccine PCV1 and rotavirus 1.
- At 10 weeks: Pentavalent 2, OPV2, PCV2 and rotavirus 2.
- At 14 weeks: Pentavalent 3, OPV3, PCV3 and inactivated polio vaccine IPV.
- At 6 months: vitamin A supplement, then every six months at the clinic or during campaigns.
- At 9 months: measles vaccine, yellow fever vaccine and meningitis A vaccine.
- At 15 months: second measles vaccine.
The Pentavalent vaccine protects against five diseases: diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus, hepatitis B and Haemophilus influenzae type b.
The BCG vaccine protects against severe forms of tuberculosis. Measles vaccine may be given as measles-rubella in areas where it has been introduced.

## The child health card
Keep your baby's immunization card safe and bring it to every visit. The health worker records each vaccine on it.
The schedule can be updated by the NPHCDA, so follow your health worker's advice.

## Missed a visit
If you miss a vaccine visit, go as soon as you can. In most cases the schedule continues where it stopped and does not start again.
Ask the health worker which vaccines your baby still needs.

## After vaccination
A mild fever, fussiness, or a small sore red spot where the injection went in is common for a day or two.
Breastfeed more often, dress your baby lightly and keep the baby comfortable. Ask the health worker what to do about fever.
Oral polio vaccine is given as drops in the mouth. A swelling at the BCG injection site that forms a small sore and heals into a scar is expected.

## When to see a health worker
Take your baby to a health facility immediately if, after a vaccine, there is a fit, difficulty breathing, swelling of the face, a high fever that does not go down, or the baby cannot be woken or will not feed.
Ask your midwife or doctor if your baby is unwell on the day of vaccination. Most minor illnesses do not stop vaccination, but a health worker should decide.
